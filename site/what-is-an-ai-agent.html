What is an AI agent?

Three different things are being sold under one word, and the differences decide whether you get your money back. This page gives the distinction that actually matters, a four-question test you can put to any vendor, and the cases where the honest answer is that you should not buy one at all.

The one-sentence version

An AI agent is software that is given a job rather than a set of instructions.

It reads what actually arrived, decides what the right next step is, uses the software you already run to take that step, and knows when a situation is outside what it should decide on its own.

The test is not whether it uses a language model — nearly everything does now. The test is whether it can handle a case nobody anticipated when it was built.

Agent, automation, chatbot: the difference that matters

These get used interchangeably by people selling them, which is convenient for the seller and expensive for the buyer.

 What it doesWhere it fails
Automation Replays a rule somebody wrote. If this happens, do that. The first case the rule did not cover. Exceptions pile up until a person is doing the work again.
Chatbot Converses. Answers questions, often well. The moment something needs to actually happen in a real system.
Agent Reasons about the situation, then acts in your systems and owns the outcome. Anything outside the scope it was given — where a good one escalates rather than guesses.

Here is the difference in one concrete case. A customer replies to a quote with "can you do it the week after next instead, and does that change the price?"

  • The automation sees a reply, marks the quote "responded", and stops. Nobody is told there is a question in it.
  • The chatbot answers the question about pricing, possibly well, and does nothing about the date.
  • The agent reads both parts, checks the calendar, sees the new week is open, notes that the price is unchanged because the scope has not changed, drafts the reply, updates the record, and — because a date change is a commitment — puts it in front of a person before it goes out.

All three are "AI". Only one of them did the job.

What an agent actually needs to have

If you strip away the marketing, an agent is four capabilities working together. Anything missing one of them is something else wearing the word.

1. It can read the real situation

Not a form submission with tidy fields — the actual message, in the customer's own words, with the ambiguity left in. If the thing needs the input pre-structured, the structuring is the work and you are still doing it.

2. It can act in your systems

Reading is easy and nearly worthless on its own. The question to ask is which systems it can write to. An agent that can read your calendar but not book in it has moved the work, not removed it.

3. It knows what it does not know

This is the one that separates a good agent from a dangerous one. When a case falls outside its scope, it must escalate rather than guess. A system confidently doing the wrong thing at scale is worse than a system that stops and asks.

4. Its independence is adjustable

You should be able to turn its autonomy down without turning it off — start it drafting work for a human to send, and raise it once you have watched it. If the only settings are on and off, you are being asked to trust it completely on day one, which nobody should do.

Autonomy is a dial, not a switch. Every BlvkWare agent goes live at Draft.

The four questions to ask any vendor

You do not need to understand the architecture. You need four answers, and a vendor who cannot give all four is selling automation or a chatbot under a better name.

  1. Which of my systems can it write to, not just read? Vague answers here are the single most reliable warning sign.
  2. What happens when it meets a case nobody anticipated? "It figures it out" is the wrong answer. "It escalates with the reason attached" is the right one.
  3. Can I see what it did and why, for every single action? If there is no log with the reasoning in it, you cannot audit a mistake and neither can they.
  4. Can I turn its independence down without turning it off? If not, you are being asked to go from nothing to full trust in one step.

A fifth question, if money or reputation is involved: what can it do that cannot be undone, and what stops it doing that unsupervised? Sending a message and taking a payment are not reversible in the way updating a record is. An agent that treats them the same has not thought about it.

When you should not buy one

This is the part most vendors leave out, so here it is plainly. Three cases where an agent is the wrong purchase.

The job happens rarely

Setup cost is roughly fixed whatever the volume. A job that happens six times a month does not repay it, however annoying those six times are. Work out how many hours a year the job actually consumes before you price anything — most people are surprised in both directions.

The job is genuinely deterministic

If a rule really does cover every case — copy this field to that field, every time, no exceptions — then plain automation is cheaper, faster and more predictable. Reasoning is what you pay extra for, and you should only pay for it where there is something to reason about.

The process is not written down anywhere

If a job exists only in one person's head, nobody can hand it to an agent until it is written down. That writing-down is most of the value and you can capture it without buying anything. Do that first; you may find you have solved the problem, and if you have not, you now have a specification instead of a wish.

The arithmetic, before anyone quotes you

Take the job you have in mind and answer three things:

1. How many times does it happen in a month?
2. How many minutes does it take each time, honestly, including the switching cost of remembering to do it?
3. What is an hour of the person currently doing it worth?

Multiply them. That is the annual cost of the job as it stands. If it is not several times the annual cost of the agent, the agent is not the right purchase — and any vendor who argues with that arithmetic is telling you something about themselves.

The second number is usually the one people get wrong. A five-minute task done eleven times a day is not fifty-five minutes; it is most of a morning, because each interruption costs more than the task.
