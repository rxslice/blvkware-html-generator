What should an AI agent cost?

Almost nobody in this market publishes a price, which makes it hard to tell a fair quote from an opportunistic one. This page gives the three pricing models you will meet, defensible ranges for each, the arithmetic for deciding, and the five things in a quote that should make you walk. Our own numbers are at the bottom, stated plainly.

You should be quoted two numbers, not one

An agent has a build cost and a running cost, and a quote that hides one inside the other is hiding something.

The build is real engineering: connecting your systems, loading your prices and policies, configuring the judgment, testing it against your actual history. The running cost is genuinely ongoing — models charge every time the agent reasons, vendors change their APIs without asking, and the agent's judgment needs correcting as your business changes.

A single figure with no ongoing fee means one of two things. Either the running costs are buried in an inflated build price, or nobody has thought about them and you will meet them later as a surprise.

The three pricing models you will meet

ModelWhat happensWhose risk
Hourly development An agency bills for time. Common from software shops moving into agents. Yours. Scope grows in the dark and the final number is unknowable at the start.
Per-seat SaaS A platform charges per user per month and you configure it yourself. Yours, differently. Cheap to start, but you are the one operating it — which is the problem you were trying to solve.
Flat build + operations A fixed price for a defined job, plus a fixed monthly to keep it running. Theirs. If it takes longer than expected, that is the builder's problem.

We use the third, so treat that as a disclosed interest rather than a neutral judgment. The argument for it is simple: it is the only one of the three where the person who controls the scope also carries the risk of getting it wrong.

What actually drives the price

Four things, in order of how much they matter. If a quote does not vary with these, it has not been thought about.

  1. How many of your systems it must operate. Each connection is real work — authentication, field mapping, error handling, and the repairs when the vendor changes something. This is usually the single biggest driver.
  2. Which channels it works on. Email is straightforward. Web chat is easy. A phone line is a different discipline, and in the United States business texting needs carrier registration that takes weeks and is outside anybody's control.
  3. How much judgment the job needs. Following a rule is cheap. Deciding what to do about a case nobody anticipated, and knowing when not to decide, is what you are actually paying for.
  4. Volume. Models charge per use, so an agent thinking ten thousand times a month costs more to run than one thinking two thousand times. Ask how volume is priced before you sign, not after.

Compare it to a person, not to software

This is where most buyers go wrong, in both directions.

Compare an agent to the seat it fills, not to the software licence beside it.

Set against a $50-a-month subscription, every agent looks expensive. Set against the $2,500–$4,000 a month a part-time person actually costs once you load it, the same agent looks obvious. Neither framing is dishonest; they are just answering different questions.

The comparison that matters most is the third one: what the job costs you by not being done. For most small businesses that is the largest number on the page, and it is the one nobody puts in a spreadsheet because it never appears on an invoice.

The arithmetic, before anybody quotes you

A  times the job happens per month
B  minutes it really takes, including remembering to do it
C  value of an hour of whoever does it now

(A × B ÷ 60) × C × 12  = what the job costs you a year

If that is not several times the agent's annual cost, do not buy one. And if a vendor argues with this arithmetic rather than helping you fill it in, that tells you something worth knowing.

B is the number people get wrong. A five-minute task done eleven times a day is not fifty-five minutes; it is most of a morning, because each interruption costs more than the task itself.

For a job that never gets done at all — quotes nobody chases, invoices nobody follows — run it the other way. Count what is being lost, multiply by a realistic recovery rate, and be conservative. Anybody quoting you a recovery percentage before they have built anything is guessing, including us.

Five warning signs in a quote

1. An hourly rate with no cap. The final number is unknowable at the point you have to decide.

2. No ongoing fee at all. Running costs are real. If they are not on the quote, they are either inside the build price or waiting for you later.

3. No price without a call. Sometimes genuine. Often it means the price depends on what you appear able to pay.

4. A promised percentage. "Recover 40% of lost quotes" cannot be known before building it for your business. Arithmetic you can check beats a statistic you cannot.

5. No stated exit. Ask what happens to your data and your configuration when you stop paying. If there is no clear answer, get one in writing before you start.
