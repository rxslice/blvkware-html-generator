The short answer. A US private-industry employee costs about 1.43 times their salary once benefits are counted. A scoped agent is typically a build fee plus a monthly fee, in our case $3,500 plus $349 a month. The agent wins when the work is high volume, rule-shaped and already written down. The person wins when volume is low, judgment is constant, or nobody has ever written the process down. Most jobs are a mix, which is why the useful question is never "person or agent" but "which hours of this job".

What is an AI employee?

It is a marketing term, not a category of software. What is being sold is an AI agent scoped to one job: software given a job rather than a list of instructions, which reads what actually arrived, works out the next step, operates the business software you already run, and escalates what it should not decide alone.

Nobody is employed. There is no contract of employment, no notice period, no employment law and no unfair dismissal. That cuts both ways, and it is worth being clear about which way in each direction:

    The statistic everybody misquotes

    You will read, on page after page, that "MIT found only 23% of jobs are economically viable to automate". It is repeated by vendors on both sides of the argument, and it is not what the study says.

    What the 2024 MIT study actually found. It examined tasks that could be done with computer vision, and concluded that about 23% of the wages paid for those vision tasks would be economically viable to automate with computer vision at then-current costs. It was not a finding about all jobs, and it was not about language-model agents, which barely feature in it.

    The transferable point is the one worth keeping, and it is genuinely important: technically possible and economically sensible are different questions, and the gap between them is mostly the cost of building and running the thing. That is exactly the gap this page is asking you to measure.

    We are pointing this out against our own interest in one direction and for it in another, which is roughly the definition of a fact worth citing. If you quote the figure, quote it as what it is: vision tasks, 2024 costs, wages rather than roles.

    When the answer is a person

    These are the cases where we tell people not to buy, and we would rather say it here than on a call in six weeks:

    • Low volume. If the task happens a few times a week, the build fee will never amortise. Ten minutes of somebody's day is not a business case.
    • Judgment every time. If no two cases are alike, there is no rule to encode and you are buying a very expensive way to generate exceptions.
    • The process is not written down. Software cannot enforce a process nobody has articulated. Write it down first. That exercise alone often fixes the problem, and it costs nothing.
    • Nobody follows the process today. A system that enforces an ignored process does not create compliance, it creates resentment and a workaround.
    • The documents contradict each other. Software makes disagreement permanent rather than resolving it. Settle it first.

    When the answer is an agent

    • Volume with a shape. The same kind of thing arriving many times a week, where the right response follows from what arrived.
    • Hours nobody wants. Evenings, weekends and the first hour of the morning, where the cost of a slow reply is a lost job rather than an inconvenience.
    • Work that is already documented. If you have an SOP, a template set or a script, most of the build is done and you are paying somebody to encode it.
    • A queue that is quietly costing you. Quotes that were never chased, invoices nobody followed up, enquiries answered two days late.

    What to check before you buy one, from anybody

    Six questions. Any vendor who cannot answer them plainly is selling you a demo:

    1. What exactly does it own, and what does it escalate? A defined job description, not "handles your email".
    2. What is it allowed to do without asking? Read, draft, or send. Those are three very different purchases. Ours are set per action and are covered in permissions.
    3. How do I see what it did, and what it refused to do? If the answer is a log file, that is not an answer. See how one is kept under control.
    4. How do I stop it? There should be an off switch a non-technical person can reach in seconds.
    5. What is the acceptance test? The specific, checkable thing that proves it works before you pay for it working.
    6. What is the total first-year cost? Build, monthly, and anything metered. Ours is printed and totalled at the configurator before you decide.
