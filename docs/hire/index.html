The questions people actually ask

If yours isn't here, email it. You'll get a straight answer, not a sales call.

Why does the tier change on its own instead of me picking it?

Because letting a buyer pick the tier means either overselling a small job or underselling a large one, and both end badly. The tier is worked out from what you've actually configured — how many systems it has to operate, how many channels it works across, and how much judgment the job needs. The panel tells you which one you're in and exactly why.

If it says Deputy and you wanted Operator money, take something out and watch it flip back. That's not a trick — a smaller scope genuinely costs less, and you can add the rest later at the same published prices.

What is an "action", and what happens if we go over?

One action is one completed task step the agent takes on your behalf — sending a follow-up, booking a slot, drafting a reply, updating a record, making a call. You picked a rough volume above and the monthly covers that.

Going over doesn't break anything and nothing gets switched off. It's $75 per additional thousand, and you're told before it happens rather than after. Buying the volume up front is cheaper than overage, which is why the bands exist.

Can all of this start today, or is something waiting on approval?

Everything on email, web chat, a shared inbox, Slack or Teams starts immediately. Inbound phone also starts immediately — answering calls needs a number, not a registration.

The one genuine exception is SMS. Texting your customers from a US business number requires A2P 10DLC carrier registration, and part of it is a manual review nobody can hurry. Budget weeks rather than days. It's flagged on the channel itself above rather than buried down here, because finding that out after ordering is exactly how a fixed timeline becomes a lie.

The practical answer is to start without it and add SMS when the registration clears — same published price, no new project.

Why is there a monthly fee — didn't I just buy the thing?

You didn't buy a thing, you hired a worker. An agent runs on models that cost money every time it thinks, it operates systems whose vendors change them without warning, and it makes decisions that need watching and correcting as your business changes.

Agent Operations covers hosting, model costs, monitoring, unlimited tuning of its logic and its language, repairs when an integration breaks, business-hours support, a monthly performance report and a quarterly review. Software you have to operate has a maintenance fee. This has a payroll.

What if the agent does something wrong?

It ships at Draft, where it can't act on its own at all — it prepares the work and a person sends it. It only acts unsupervised at a level you turn on in writing, after you've watched it work, and you can turn it back down instantly. Before it touches a live customer it rehearses against your real history taking no action, and you read that log first.

Every action is logged with what it saw, what it decided, why, and what it did. Where the connected system supports undoing something the agent records how before it acts, so it can be rolled back. A message somebody has read cannot be recalled by anyone, which is why sending is the thing Draft holds. You can also stop it yourself in one press from the page that comes with it. Where your credentials live, what leaves your systems, and how you stop it is written out in full. The commercial answer is in the terms and isn't hidden: liability is capped at fees paid. Any provider telling you their agent can't make a mistake is either lying or hasn't shipped one.

What if I stop paying?

The agent pauses and your data is exported to you within five business days, all of it, in a format you can use. If you'd rather keep running it yourself, the source is available at twelve times the monthly fee.

That's published on purpose, because "you'll never get your business back out" is the fair objection to every managed service and it deserves an answer in writing rather than a reassurance on a call.

What if it doesn't work?

For an Operator: thirty days. If it doesn't do the job it was hired for, the build fee comes back.

For a Deputy the guarantee is stronger in the way that matters more — the acceptance tests you can see in your specification are written into the job description before work starts, and work continues at no extra charge until they pass. That's deliberately not a refund, because a refund lets a supplier walk away from a hard build. This one doesn't let me.

Who actually builds it?

One person — William Russell Wheeler, from Mississippi, working with businesses across the United States. The same person who reads your order, writes your job description, builds the agent and picks up when something breaks. No account manager, no handoff, no offshore team.

That's why the scope and the timeline are fixed rather than open-ended, and why there's a catalog rather than an infinite menu. It's also a real risk to you, which is why the export and the source buyout above exist.
